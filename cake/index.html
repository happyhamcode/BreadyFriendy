Cake, worked out by ratio

Pick pound, butter, sponge or chiffon, enter what you have, and get every gram. Every amount is a percentage of the flour, so a recipe scales to any pan and any size.

What is a baker's percentage?

Every ingredient is written as a percentage of the flour's weight, so flour is always 100%. Cakes are measured in grams, because a cup of flour can vary by 30 g or more between bakers.

Example: 250 g flour and 125 g butter: 125 ÷ 250 = 50% butter. Double the flour and every other amount doubles with it.

That is the whole trick behind scaling, swapping and fixing recipes: change one percentage and you can see exactly what you changed.

Why balance matters

A cake works because a few jobs are shared out. Flour and eggs give structure. Fat and sugar make it tender. Liquid dissolves the sugar and makes steam. Baking powder or whipped eggs lift it. Too much of one and the others cannot keep up.

Leave something blank and Bake by Math fills in a typical amount for the cake type you chose, listed on each card below. If the numbers drift out of balance, you get a plain warning instead of a failed cake.

Four kinds of cake

Pound cake

Equal weights of flour, butter, sugar and eggs (1:1:1:1), the original recipe. Dense, fine and buttery, with no baking powder: creamed butter and eggs carry the rise.

  • Butter 100%
  • Sugar 100%
  • Whole egg 100%
  • Salt 1%
  • Vanilla 2%

Left blank, we use these percentages of flour.

Butter cake

Creamed butter and sugar, eggs, milk and baking powder: the everyday layer, sheet or cupcake. A soft, tender crumb with a high sugar and liquid share.

  • Butter 50%
  • Sugar 110%
  • Whole egg 50%
  • Milk 70%
  • Baking powder 4.5%
  • Salt 1.5%
  • Vanilla 2%

Sponge (genoise)

Whole eggs whipped with sugar carry all the lift. Very little fat and no baking powder. Light and springy, often brushed with syrup.

  • Sugar 100%
  • Whole egg 160%
  • Butter 25%
  • Salt 0.5%
  • Vanilla 2%

Chiffon

Oil, yolks and water mixed into the flour, then folded into whipped whites, plus baking powder. Tender like a butter cake, light like a sponge, and it stays moist.

  • Sugar 110%
  • Oil 40%
  • Egg yolk 45%
  • Egg white 100%
  • Water 65%
  • Baking powder 4%
  • Salt 1.5%
  • Vanilla 2%

What each ingredient does

Sugar

Does: sweetens, holds water so the cake stays moist, tenderizes by delaying the set of the batter, browns the crust, and helps whipped eggs keep their air.

Use when: always. Most cakes have sugar at 100% of the flour or more.

Butter

Does: fat coats the flour proteins so the crumb is tender. Creamed with sugar, it traps air that expands in the oven. One US stick is 113 g.

Use when: you want flavor and a fine crumb. Pound and butter cakes depend on it.

Oil

Does: pure fat, no water. It stays liquid when cold, so oil cakes stay soft from the fridge. It cannot be creamed, so the lift comes from baking powder or whipped eggs.

Use when: chiffon, or when you want a moist cake that is quick to mix.

Whole egg

Does: the protein sets to give structure, the yolk fat and lecithin hold the batter together, and the water makes steam. 50 g per large egg.

Use when: always. Eggs are the structure of a butter cake and the lift of a sponge.

Egg yolk

Does: more fat and less water than a whole egg. Richer color, flavor and a fine crumb. 17 g per large yolk.

Use when: chiffon and rich cakes.

Egg white

Does: almost all water and protein. Whipped, it makes a foam that lifts chiffon and angel-style cakes. 33 g per large white.

Use when: you want a light, airy crumb.

Milk

Does: water to dissolve the sugar and make steam, with a little fat. Milk proteins and sugar help the crust brown.

Use when: butter cakes, or to loosen a stiff batter.

Buttermilk

Does: a tangy, acidic liquid. The acid softens the crumb and reacts with baking soda to make gas.

Use when: red velvet, chocolate and any recipe that uses baking soda.

Sour cream

Does: acidic like buttermilk, with about 19% fat. A moist, tender, slightly tangy crumb.

Use when: a dense, moist cake such as coffee cake or pound cake.

Cocoa (natural)

Does: adds flavor, color and about 14% fat. Natural cocoa is acidic, so it reacts with baking soda. Cocoa soaks up liquid like flour does.

Use when: chocolate cakes. Dutch-processed cocoa is alkalized and not acidic, so it needs baking powder instead of soda.

Baking powder

Does: baking soda plus a dry acid. It releases gas when wet and again in the oven. About 1 to 1½ tsp per cup of flour is typical.

Use when: butter cakes and chiffon. Old powder goes flat.

Baking soda

Does: makes gas only when it meets an acid such as buttermilk, sour cream or natural cocoa. Too much, or with no acid, leaves a soapy taste.

Use when: your recipe already has an acid.
