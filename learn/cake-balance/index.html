Cake Balance: The Ratio Rules

A cake recipe is a balance. Flour and eggs build structure. Fat and sugar tenderize. Liquid dissolves the sugar and makes steam. Leavening lifts. Change one and the others have to follow.

Pound cake: 1:1:1:1

The classic pound cake is equal weights of flour, butter, sugar and eggs. Many modern recipes drift from it. One published formula, for example, has sugar at 125% of the flour rather than 100%.

High-ratio cakes

A high-ratio cake has at least as much sugar as flour by weight, and the liquid (eggs plus any milk or other liquid) weighs at least as much as the sugar. The extra liquid is there to dissolve all that sugar. These cakes usually use cake flour and an emulsifier, such as egg yolks, to hold the batter together.

What the calculator checks

  • Butter cake: sugar at least equal to flour, liquid at least equal to sugar, eggs not far below the butter.
  • Pound cake: butter, sugar and eggs each within 80 to 120% of the flour.
  • Sponge: enough whole egg to whip into the lift, at least 120% of the flour.
  • Chiffon: whites at least equal to yolks.
  • All cakes: fat and baking powder within the usual range for that type, and baking soda only with an acid such as buttermilk, sour cream or natural cocoa.

About 1 to 1½ teaspoons of baking powder per cup of flour (3.3 to 5% by weight) is a common rule of thumb. The fat ranges for each cake type are typical home-baking values, not a published standard. Treat every warning as a prompt to look, not a verdict.

What goes wrong when it drifts

Sugar that has too little liquid to dissolve tends to bake dry and coarse. Too little egg for the amount of butter can leave the cake greasy or crumbly. Baking soda without an acid tastes soapy. Too much baking powder can make the cake rise and then fall.
