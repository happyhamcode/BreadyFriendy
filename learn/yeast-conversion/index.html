Yeast Conversion: Instant, Active Dry and Fresh

Instant, active dry and fresh (cake) yeast are the same organism in different forms. Fresh has the most water, so you need more of it by weight.

What the sources say

Sources disagree on instant versus active dry, and each position has a published basis.

  • America's Test Kitchen tested breads of several kinds and says to use 25% more active dry yeast than instant, to make up for the dead cells in active dry.
  • Bob's Red Mill says to multiply the instant amount by 1.25 to get active dry, and to use three times the weight of fresh yeast in place of instant, or 2.5 times in place of active dry.
  • King Arthur says the two can be swapped in equal amounts, but that active dry is slower to start, and to allow 15 to 20 minutes more on a one-hour rise. Its reference page gives fresh yeast as 0.4 times the amount in active dry yeast and 0.33 times the amount in instant, which works out to about 1.2 times as much active dry as instant.
  • Red Star says that in traditional kneaded doughs you may use them interchangeably, one for one.

Fresh (cake) yeast: King Arthur gives 0.33 for instant and 0.4 for active dry, so fresh is about 3 times the instant weight. A 0.6 ounce (17 g) cake is about one 7 g packet of dry yeast.

What Bake by Math uses

Bake by Math uses the ratio 1 : 1.25 : 3 for instant, active dry and fresh. That is the America's Test Kitchen and Bob's Red Mill figure, and it agrees with King Arthur's fresh yeast factors. For 500 g of flour at the recommended amounts that is 10 g instant yeast (2%), 12.5 g active dry (2.5%) or 30 g fresh (6%).

If you follow the one-for-one advice, enter the same grams for both types. The two will both rise, and the active dry dough will simply be a little slower.

How much yeast

The recommended 2% instant yeast sits in the middle of King Arthur's same-day enriched recipes, which use between 1.4% and 2.9% of the flour weight (pretzels 1.4%, challah 1.6%, sandwich bread 1.7%, cinnamon rolls 1.7%, brioche 2.7%, milk bread 2.9%). Slower, cooler rises use less. Lean doughs fermented overnight use far less, around 0.7% in King Arthur's ciabatta.

Which to use

Instant yeast can be mixed straight into the flour. Active dry yeast is usually dissolved in warm water first. Fresh yeast is crumbled in and needs refrigeration. Fresh yeast also contains water, about 70%, which the calculator counts toward hydration.
